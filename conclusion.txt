DEEP DIVE — FAILING POINTS
---------------------------

1. R25 — BRAND NAME CONSISTENCY (91.7% fail rate)
   The most universally broken checkpoint. Stores present their brand name
   differently across og:site_name tags, footer text, and schema.org markup.
   Minimum consistency scores ranged from 8.7% (Death Wish Coffee) to 43.5%
   (The Sill). This is an AI-readability issue: inconsistent brand identity
   across crawlable surfaces confuses LLMs that try to attribute product data
   to a specific brand entity.

   Most common mismatch pattern: og_site_name vs. footer text vs. schema.org
   label all carry slightly different capitalizations, abbreviations, or
   suffixes (e.g. "Morphe US" vs. "Morphe" vs. plain-text footer).

   Only huel.com showed minor variation (warn, not fail) with a 72.7%
   consistency floor. Only hismileteeth.com escaped scoring (1 source only).

2. R16 — RETURN/REFUND POLICY EXTRACTABILITY (87.5% fail rate)
   21 of 24 stores have either a missing refund policy page or a policy that
   exists but contains no concrete timeframe that can be machine-extracted.
   AI assistants cannot cite a return window they cannot find.

   The 3 stores that passed: skims.com ("return within 30 days"), huel.com
   ("30-Day Money Back"), deathwishcoffee.com ("60 day money back"). The
   common thread: short, explicit, surface-level text — not buried in legal
   paragraphs.

   Gymshark and Ruggable were worst: their refund policy page was not found
   at all (404 or crawler-inaccessible URL).

3. R9 — PRICE SIGNAL ON HOMEPAGE (66.7% fail rate)
   16 stores display no crawler-visible price on the homepage. AI shopping
   assistants use price signals to anchor recommendations. A store with no
   visible price is invisible to price-sensitive queries.

   Passing stores exposed at least one currency-formatted price in raw HTML
   (not behind JS rendering), e.g. gymshark ($75), huel ($65), brooklinen
   ($25–$75), deathwishcoffee ($,). Homepage price placement is a quick win.

4. R17 — SHIPPING POLICY TIMEFRAME EXTRACTABILITY (66.7% fail rate)
   16 stores either have no accessible shipping policy or the policy exists
   but expresses no parseable timeframe. Like return policies, if an AI
   can't extract a number (e.g. "3–5 business days"), it can't answer
   shipping-related shopping queries.

   Passing stores had explicit text: fashionnova ("1-12 business days"),
   kith ("3-5 business days"), fentybeauty ("4-8 business days"), rarebeauty
   ("1-3 business days"), flybyjing ("2-4 business days"), thesill
   ("2-6 business days"), glossier ("express shipping").

5. R31 — GMC READINESS SIGNALS (45.8% fail, 33.3% warn)
   Only 5 stores (21%) passed Google Merchant Center readiness signals. 11
   stores showed only 0–1/4 signals (FAIL), and 8 showed 2/4 (WARN). The
   most commonly missing signals: og:type=product markup, currency-formatted
   prices, and google-site-verification. Ruggable and madeincookware were
   the weakest — 0/4 signals.

6. R7 — COMMERCE SCHEMA (schema.org) (20.8% fail rate)
   5 stores had zero schema.org markup at all: allbirds.com, hismileteeth.com,
   liquiddeath.com, ruggable.com, madeincookware.com. 2 stores had schema
   but without commerce-relevant types (dunelondon had only WebPage; glossier
   had Product types but minimal context). Commerce schema (Organization,
   WebSite, Product) is fundamental AI crawl infrastructure.

SYSTEMIC ISSUES
---------------
  1. Homepage price stripping: Most stores render prices client-side (React/
     JS hydration). AI crawlers see the DOM before JS runs, missing prices
     entirely. A server-side or SSG price render in raw HTML would fix R9
     for 16 stores.

  2. Policy language is AI-hostile: Policy pages often use hedging language
     ("may vary", "typically", "subject to change") instead of explicit dates
     and ranges. AI systems extract confidence-weighted text — vague policies
     extract as nothing.

  3. Brand name fragmentation: Organizations have different presentation
     layers (marketing copy, OG tags, schema labels) managed by different
     teams, creating inconsistency that scores poorly on R25. A brand name
     style guide enforced at the schema.org and OG meta level would fix this.

  4. Shopify dependency creates false security: Shopify auto-passes R1, R3,
     R28, R30 for all stores. Brands treat their Shopify setup as "AI-ready"
     when the actual content signals (schema richness, policy depth, price
     visibility) are entirely within their control and broadly failing.


PRIORITY ACTION LIST (Applies to most stores)
----------------------------------------------
  [HIGH]   Fix brand name consistency across og:site_name, schema.org, and
           footer. Pick one canonical form and apply it everywhere.

  [HIGH]   Add explicit return timeframe to homepage or policy page in
           plain, extractable text (e.g., "Free returns within 30 days").

  [HIGH]   Add explicit shipping timeframe in plain text to policy page
           (e.g., "Ships in 3-5 business days").

  [MEDIUM] Expose at least one currency-formatted price in server-rendered
           HTML on the homepage. Even a "From $X" line helps R9.

  [MEDIUM] Add google-site-verification meta tag and og:type=product on
           product pages to improve R31 GMC signals.

  [LOW]    Audit schema.org markup. Stores without any schema (allbirds,
           liquiddeath, ruggable, madeincookware) need at minimum Organization
           + WebSite types added to all pages.


CONCLUSION
----------
The batch shows a category-wide structural readiness gap for AI shopping
assistants. Stores are technically accessible (AI crawlers are not blocked,
sitemaps are present, Shopify infrastructure works) but content-layer signals
are missing at scale. The three highest-impact, lowest-effort fixes are:
(1) normalizing brand name presentation, (2) writing machine-readable policy
text with explicit timeframes, and (3) surfacing at least one price in
server-rendered HTML on the homepage.

The 4 VALIDATED stores (huel, fentybeauty, rarebeauty, deathwishcoffee)
demonstrate the pattern clearly — they share the same Shopify infrastructure
as the failing stores but differ in content decisions: visible prices, clear
policy language, and consistent brand identity. The gap is not technical; it
is editorial.
